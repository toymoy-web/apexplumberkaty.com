Garbage Disposal Troubleshooting Before You Call a Plumber

By the Katy Apex Plumbing team · September 21, 2026

A good number of garbage disposal calls turn out to be something a homeowner could have resolved in five minutes. Here's what's worth trying before you schedule a repair, and what genuinely needs a plumber, so you're not paying for a visit that a quick check at home could have resolved on its own.

If It Hums but Doesn't Spin

This almost always means a jam, something is physically blocking the flywheel from turning, not a dead motor. Turn off power at the switch and breaker first, then use a hex key (most disposals include one, or a standard Allen wrench works) in the small hole on the bottom of the unit to manually rotate the flywheel back and forth until it turns freely.

Once it spins freely by hand, restore power and try the reset button on the bottom of the unit before turning it on. This resolves a genuinely large share of humming-disposal calls without needing a service visit at all.

If It Doesn't Respond at All

Before assuming the unit is dead, check the reset button on the bottom of the disposal, a small red or black button that trips when the motor overheats. Push it firmly and try the switch again. If that doesn't work, check the breaker, since a disposal is often on its own dedicated circuit that can trip without affecting anything else in the kitchen.

If power is confirmed at the outlet and breaker and the unit still won't respond, that points to an internal electrical or motor issue that does need a plumber, not something safely fixable with more troubleshooting.

Signs You Shouldn't Try to Fix It Yourself

Any leaking from the bottom of the unit means the housing itself has likely cracked, and that's a replacement, not a repair, regardless of how minor the leak looks. Continuing to run a leaking disposal risks water damage to the cabinet below for no benefit, since the leak won't seal itself.

A grinding or rattling noise that wasn't there before can mean something lodged in the unit that shouldn't be there, a piece of glass, a bone fragment, or utensil. Turn it off immediately rather than running it repeatedly trying to clear the sound, since that risks damaging the motor or blades further.

Preventing the Next Jam

Most disposal jams trace back to a small number of repeat offenders: fibrous foods like celery and corn husks, starchy foods like potato peels that turn pasty, and grease or fat that solidifies inside the unit. Running cold water while the disposal operates, and for a bit after, helps flush waste through rather than letting it settle and harden inside the unit.

Avoid the temptation to use a disposal as a general trash disposal for anything that fits down the drain. It's built for small food scraps, not fibrous plant material, bones, or anything that isn't genuinely soft food waste, and treating it as a catch-all is the single most common cause of repeat jams.

How Often a Disposal Actually Needs Replacing

A well-maintained disposal, run properly and not abused with the wrong waste, typically lasts eight to twelve years before the motor genuinely wears out rather than just jamming occasionally. If you're troubleshooting the same unit repeatedly within just a couple of years of installation, that's less likely to be normal wear and worth having a plumber assess whether the original unit or installation was actually adequate for the household's use.

A unit replaced during a kitchen remodel or as part of new construction should reasonably be expected to last most of a decade without repeat issues, so a pattern of trouble sooner than that is a real signal, not just bad luck.

A Few Things That Are Actually Fine to Put Down It

Despite common myths, small amounts of soft food scraps, cooked vegetables, small fruit pieces, and similar soft waste are exactly what a disposal is designed to handle, and running it regularly with appropriate waste actually keeps it working better than rarely using it at all. Ice cubes run through occasionally can help clean the blades, contrary to some advice suggesting otherwise.

The goal isn't avoiding the disposal entirely out of fear of jamming it, it's understanding the specific handful of materials, fibrous, starchy, greasy, that cause the most trouble, and routing those to the trash instead while using the disposal normally for everything else.

When a Repair Visit Is Actually the Cheaper Option

Repeatedly troubleshooting the same jam week after week costs real time, and if it's happening often enough that you've started keeping the hex key somewhere handy, that frequency itself is a sign something's actually wrong rather than just occasional bad luck with food scraps. A repair visit that identifies and fixes the underlying cause, a worn motor bearing, a consistently undersized unit for the household, saves more time over a year than repeated five-minute fixes.

There's no shame in troubleshooting first, it genuinely resolves a lot of calls. But recognizing when troubleshooting has become a recurring chore rather than an occasional fix is worth acting on, both for the time saved and because a unit that's failing gradually rarely gets better on its own without addressing whatever's actually causing the repeat problem.

Common Questions

Why does my disposal smell bad even after cleaning?

Food debris often gets trapped where the blades can't reach it. A plumber can access those spots during a repair visit if home cleaning doesn't resolve it.

Can I use ice and citrus peels to clean my disposal?

Yes, this is a reasonable, safe way to freshen a disposal and can help with mild odor, though it won't fix a mechanical issue.

Is it normal for a disposal to be loud?

Some operating noise is normal, but a sudden increase in noise or a new rattling sound is worth having checked before it develops into a bigger mechanical issue.

How much horsepower does a typical household disposal need?

One-third to one-half horsepower covers most households. Larger families or heavy daily use may benefit from a higher-horsepower unit, which a plumber can help you size correctly for your kitchen.

Can a garbage disposal be installed under any kitchen sink?

Most standard kitchen sinks can accommodate one, though older cabinets occasionally need minor modification for clearance around the drain and trap.
