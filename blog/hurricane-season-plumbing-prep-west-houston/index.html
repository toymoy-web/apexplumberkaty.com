Hurricane Season Plumbing Prep for West Houston Homeowners

By the Katy Apex Plumbing team · September 21, 2026

West Houston's hurricane season isn't a once-a-year inconvenience, it's a recurring stress test for anything in a home's plumbing that's supposed to move water away during heavy rain. A little preparation before the season peaks is cheaper than an emergency call during it, and most of the checks involved take less time than a single load of laundry.

Sump Pumps Only Get Tested When It's Too Late

A sump pump that's never run under real load is the single most common gap we see going into storm season. Homeowners assume it works because it's installed and plugged in, but a pump can fail from a stuck float switch, a clogged intake, or a worn motor without ever showing a problem until the day it's actually needed.

Testing one is simple: pour enough water into the sump pit to trigger the float switch and confirm the pump actually kicks on and moves water out through the discharge line. Doing this once before the season, rather than during the first heavy rain, is the difference between a five-minute check and a flooded space.

Backflow Prevention Matters More Than Most Homeowners Realize

Heavy, sustained rain can overwhelm municipal storm and sewer systems, and when that happens, water can back up into a home's drains instead of flowing out. A backflow prevention device stops that reversal. Homes without one, or with one that hasn't been checked in years, are at real risk of a sewage backup during the worst storms of the season, not from anything wrong inside the house itself.

This is worth checking now rather than after a storm has already caused a backup, since a backflow issue found proactively is a straightforward installation or repair, while one found during an active storm is an emergency call layered on top of cleanup.

What Else Is Worth a Quick Look

Outdoor drainage around the foundation, gutters, downspouts, and grading, isn't strictly plumbing, but it directly affects how much water pressure a home's drainage system has to handle during a storm. Clear debris from downspouts and confirm they're actually directing water away from the foundation rather than dumping it right next to it.

Inside the house, know where the main water shutoff is before you need it in a hurry. If a pipe does fail during a storm, the ability to shut off water immediately is what keeps a bad situation from becoming a much worse one.

After the Storm: What to Check

Once the weather clears, don't assume everything is fine just because there's no obvious flooding. Check the sump pump pit for standing water that isn't draining, which can indicate the pump ran continuously and may need servicing even if it appeared to work. Inspect any exterior cleanouts for signs they were forced open or damaged by pressure during the heaviest rain.

If the home experienced any backflow or slow drainage during the storm, it's worth having a plumber check the backflow prevention device even if things seem to have returned to normal, since a device that was overwhelmed once may have sustained damage that only shows up during the next major storm.

Building a Yearly Habit, Not a One-Time Check

Gulf Coast storm season returns every year, which means this isn't a checklist to run once and forget. Testing the sump pump, checking the backflow device, and clearing drainage paths is worth doing as a standing seasonal habit each spring before the season ramps up, the same way you'd check smoke detector batteries on a schedule rather than waiting for a reason.

Homes that make this a habit tend to have far fewer storm-related plumbing emergencies than homes that only think about it once something has already gone wrong.

Why a Battery Backup for Your Sump Pump Matters Here

The worst-case scenario for a sump pump isn't the pump failing on its own, it's losing power during the exact storm that's generating the most water for it to handle. Gulf Coast storms frequently cause power outages alongside heavy rain, which means a standard sump pump with no battery backup can fail at precisely the moment it's needed most, even if the pump itself is in perfect working order.

A battery backup system keeps the pump running through a power outage for several hours, long enough to get through most storm events, and it's a relatively modest investment compared to the cost of a flooded space that a working-but-unpowered pump couldn't prevent.

For any West Houston home relying on a sump pump as a real line of defense during storm season, a battery backup isn't a luxury add-on, it's closing the most predictable failure point in the entire system. A water heater strapped or secured against movement matters too, since high winds and any structural shifting can stress connections that seemed stable under normal conditions, and confirming those connections are snug is a quick addition to the same seasonal checklist worth doing at the same time as the pump test.

Common Questions

How often should I test my sump pump?

At least once before hurricane season each year, and ideally again mid-season if the area has had a dry stretch, since a pump can seize up from disuse just as easily as from a mechanical failure.

What size battery backup does a typical home sump pump need?

It depends on the pump's draw and pit size. A plumber can size it correctly based on your specific system.

Should I turn off water to my house before evacuating for a storm?

It's a reasonable precaution if you'll be away for an extended period, since it limits damage if a line fails while no one's home to notice or respond to it quickly.

Do I need a permit to install a battery backup sump pump?

Requirements vary by jurisdiction. A plumber handling the installation typically manages any required permitting as part of the job, so it's one less thing for you to track down yourself before storm season arrives.

How long does a sump pump battery backup typically last during an outage?

Several hours in most cases, long enough to cover most storm-related outages, though exact duration depends on the specific system, battery capacity, and pit size.
