Why Your Water Bill Jumped in Katy, TX

By the Katy Apex Plumbing team · September 21, 2026

A water bill that jumps with no obvious change in household habits is one of the most common reasons Katy homeowners call a plumber, and it's almost always a leak somewhere the eye can't see, not a billing error. Working through the likely causes in order, rather than guessing, gets you to an answer faster.

The First Check You Can Do Yourself

Before assuming the worst, check the water meter with every fixture in the house turned off. Most meters have a small dial or digital indicator that moves whenever water is flowing. If it's moving with nothing running, water is going somewhere, whether that's a running toilet, an underground line, or a slab leak.

A running toilet is worth ruling out first since it's the single most common cause of a mysteriously high bill and the easiest to check. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper valve is leaking, a cheap and quick fix compared to the alternatives.

If It's Not the Toilet

Once a running toilet is ruled out, the likely candidates narrow to an underground irrigation line, a slab leak, or a leak inside a wall. Katy's clay soil makes slab leaks a real possibility in older homes, while newer construction with an irrigation system installed during the build is worth checking for a cracked or disconnected line, since irrigation leaks run constantly and quietly add up.

A leak inside a wall usually comes with a secondary sign eventually, a damp spot, a musty smell, or discoloration, but it can run for weeks before any of that becomes visible. This is where electronic leak detection earns its cost, since it locates the actual source without guessing.

Why Guessing Costs More Than Diagnosing

It's tempting to wait and see if the next bill goes back to normal, but a leak that's actually there doesn't fix itself, and every month it runs is both wasted water and, in the case of a slab leak, ongoing stress on the surrounding foundation and flooring. The cost of a proper leak detection visit is almost always smaller than a few more months of an undiagnosed leak plus the eventual repair.

If the toilet checks out clean and the bill is still unexplained, that's the point to call rather than keep guessing.

What a Normal Bill Actually Looks Like

It helps to know your household's typical usage before trying to spot an anomaly. Most water bills show usage history for the past several months directly on the statement or through the utility's online portal, which makes a sudden jump obvious against a fairly consistent baseline. A gradual creep upward over several months is worth watching just as closely as a sudden spike, since a small, constant leak produces exactly that pattern.

If your household hasn't added residents, started watering the lawn more, or changed habits in any obvious way, any meaningful increase over your normal baseline deserves the same investigation as a dramatic spike, even if it doesn't feel urgent yet.

Irrigation Systems Are an Easy Thing to Overlook

Homes with a built-in irrigation system, common across newer Katy communities, have an entire second plumbing system buried in the yard that's easy to forget about when troubleshooting a bill. A cracked line or a stuck valve in an irrigation system runs water continuously without any visible sign inside the house at all.

If your home has irrigation and the toilet checks out clean, running a manual check of each irrigation zone, watching for a section that seems to run water even when it shouldn't be active, is worth doing before assuming the issue is inside the house's plumbing at all.

How Long to Wait Before Calling Someone

If the toilet test comes back clean and there's no irrigation system to check, waiting for the next billing cycle to see if it happens again isn't unreasonable for a modest, one-time-looking increase. But if the increase is significant, more than 50 percent above your normal usage, or if it persists into a second month, that's the point to stop waiting and get a proper leak detection visit.

The reasoning is straightforward: a leak that's real doesn't fix itself between billing cycles, and every month it continues is both wasted money and, if it's a slab leak, additional stress on the foundation and flooring around it. The cost of waiting compounds in a way that the cost of checking simply doesn't.

When in doubt, the meter test costs nothing and takes fifteen minutes, so there's rarely a good reason not to at least rule out the simplest causes immediately rather than waiting to see what next month's bill looks like.

Keeping a Record Helps More Than You'd Expect

Taking a photo of your water bill each month, or jotting down the usage figure somewhere easy to check, turns a vague sense that something seems off into an actual comparison. This is especially useful across a full year, since it lets you compare a given month against the same month a year earlier, accounting naturally for seasonal changes like increased outdoor watering in summer.

This kind of simple record costs nothing and takes seconds a month, and it's often what turns a homeowner's hunch into the specific, useful detail that helps a plumber diagnose the cause faster once you do call. Even a rough mental note of your last two or three bills is better than nothing, since the goal is simply having a real baseline to compare against rather than guessing whether this month's number is actually unusual.

Common Questions

Can a faulty water meter cause a high bill?

It's uncommon, but it can happen. If your own checks find nothing at all, ask your utility to test the meter directly.

Does a pool add-fill count toward a high bill?

Yes, topping off a pool after evaporation is normal and can look like a spike if you're not already accounting for it in your monthly usage tracking.

How quickly can a leak be found once I call?

Usually the same visit, since electronic leak detection equipment locates the source quickly without extensive guesswork or unnecessary digging.

Does the utility company ever cause a false high reading?

It's uncommon but not impossible. Ruling out household causes first is the right order before questioning the meter itself.
