Toilet Running or Leaking? A Katy Homeowner's Diagnostic Guide

By the Katy Apex Plumbing team · September 21, 2026

A toilet that runs constantly or leaks somewhere is rarely a mystery once you know where to look. Most causes are inexpensive parts, the trick is figuring out which one is actually failing, which this guide walks through in the order most likely to actually apply to your toilet.

If the Toilet Runs Continuously

A continuously running toilet is almost always the flapper valve, the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that's supposed to seat tightly after each flush. Over time it warps, cracks, or accumulates enough mineral buildup that it stops sealing properly, letting water slowly leak from the tank into the bowl, which triggers the fill valve to keep topping off the tank.

The food coloring test confirms it quickly: add a few drops to the tank, wait fifteen minutes without flushing, and check the bowl. Color appearing in the bowl means the flapper is leaking. It's an inexpensive part and a straightforward replacement in most toilets.

If It Runs Intermittently

A toilet that runs briefly on its own every so often, rather than continuously, usually points to a slow leak past the flapper that only triggers the fill valve once enough water has escaped to drop below a threshold. This is the same root cause as continuous running, just a slower version of it, and the same flapper check applies.

If the flapper checks out fine and it's still happening, the fill valve itself may not be shutting off completely, which is a slightly less common but still simple part to replace.

If Water Is Visible on the Floor

Water pooling around the base of the toilet is a different problem from a running toilet, and it's worth taking seriously since it can indicate a failing wax ring seal between the toilet and the drain flange. A failing wax ring lets water (and eventually sewage) leak underneath the toilet with every flush, which can damage subflooring if it goes unaddressed.

Water coming from the supply line connection at the wall is a simpler fix, usually a loose or worn connection rather than anything involving the toilet's seal to the floor. Tightening the connection sometimes resolves it, but a persistent leak there is worth a proper repair rather than repeated tightening.

How Much a Running Toilet Actually Costs You

A continuously running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons of water per day depending on how badly the flapper is leaking, which adds up to a real, noticeable increase on a water bill within a single billing cycle. Unlike a hidden slab leak, this is one of the more expensive-per-day problems in a home precisely because it's constant and often runs for weeks before anyone investigates the sound.

The fix costing under the price of a takeout dinner in most cases makes this one of the highest-value simple repairs available, both for the water saved and for the fact that a flapper left to fail completely can eventually prevent the toilet from filling properly at all.

When It's More Than Just the Flapper

If you've replaced the flapper and the toilet still runs or refills unexpectedly, the fill valve itself may need replacement, or the flush handle's chain might be tangled or too short, preventing the flapper from sealing even though the part itself is fine. These are still simple, inexpensive fixes, just different parts than the first, most common cause.

A toilet that's old enough to be original to an older Katy home may simply be at the point where full internal replacement of the tank's components makes more sense than repeatedly swapping individual parts as each one fails in turn.

Older Toilets and Water Efficiency

A toilet original to a pre-1990s Katy home may predate modern low-flow standards entirely, using significantly more water per flush than a current model. Beyond any running or leaking issue, replacing a genuinely old toilet with a modern low-flow model can meaningfully reduce water use on its own, independent of fixing any specific leak, which is worth factoring into the repair-versus-replace decision for an older unit.

This isn't necessary for every older toilet, plenty function fine for years with basic part replacement, but it's a real option worth considering when a toilet is already due for its second or third internal repair.

A Simple Way to Confirm the Repair Worked

After replacing a flapper or fill valve, run the food coloring test again the same way as the initial diagnosis, waiting fifteen minutes without flushing and checking for color in the bowl. This confirms the repair actually solved the problem rather than assuming it did just because the sound of running water stopped, since a partial fix can quiet the noise without fully stopping a slow leak.

It's a two-minute check that provides real confidence the issue is resolved, rather than finding out it wasn't when the next water bill arrives.

Common Questions

Can a running toilet actually run up my bill significantly?

Yes, a bad flapper can waste hundreds of gallons a day, a real and noticeable cost within one billing cycle.

Is it normal for a toilet to run briefly after flushing?

Yes, refilling the tank for a few seconds is normal. Running well beyond that, or restarting on its own after it stops, generally isn't.

Can I replace a flapper myself?

Many homeowners do, since it's a simple part. If it doesn't resolve the issue, the fill valve or flange may need a plumber's professional attention instead.

Why does my toilet make a high-pitched sound while filling?

That's typically the fill valve itself wearing out, a straightforward and inexpensive part for a plumber to replace during a routine visit.
